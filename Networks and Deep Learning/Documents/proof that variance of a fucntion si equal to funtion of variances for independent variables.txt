 Nielsen Exercise — Variance of the Weighted Sum

Yes — your derivation of the **mean** is correct, and you're approaching the variance in exactly the right way.

 There is one crucial point, though:

 > The statement\
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(X_1+\cdots+X_n)
> =
> \operatorname{Var}(X_1)+\cdots+\operatorname{Var}(X_n)
> $$
>
> \
>  is **not true for arbitrary random variables**. It is true when the random variables are **independent** (more generally, when their covariances are zero).

 That's why Nielsen explicitly gives you the hint about independent random variables.

 Let's derive it from scratch.

 ## 1\. Start with your neuron

 You have

 $$
z=\sum_j w_jx_j+b.
$$

 In Nielsen's exercise, the $x_j$'s are treated as fixed values, while the $w_j$'s and $b$ are random variables.

 Since the $x_j$'s are constants, define

 $$
X_j=w_jx_j.
$$

 Then

 $$
z=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n+b.
$$

 We want

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(z).
$$

---

 ## 2\. Start from the definition of variance

 The variance of a random variable $Z$ is

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(Z)
=
E[(Z-E[Z])^2].
$$

 You already showed that

 $$
E[z]=\sum_j E[w_j]x_j+E[b].
$$

 Therefore

 $$
z-E[z]
=
\sum_j w_jx_j+b
-
\left(
\sum_j E[w_j]x_j+E[b]
\right).
$$

 Rearrange:

 $$
z-E[z]
=
\sum_j
\left(w_j-E[w_j]\right)x_j
+
\left(b-E[b]\right).
$$

 For readability, let's call

 $$
\tilde w_j=w_j-E[w_j]
$$

 and

 $$
\tilde b=b-E[b].
$$

 Then

 $$
z-E[z]
=
\sum_j \tilde w_jx_j+\tilde b.
$$

 So the variance is

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
E\left[
\left(
\sum_j\tilde w_jx_j+\tilde b
\right)^2
\right].
$$

 And **this is where the important part happens**.

---

 # 3\. Expand the square

 Let's temporarily consider just two weights:

 $$
z=w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b.
$$

 Then

 $$
z-E[z]
=
\tilde w_1x_1+\tilde w_2x_2+\tilde b.
$$

 Squaring:

 $$
(z-E[z])^2
=
\tilde w_1^2x_1^2
+
\tilde w_2^2x_2^2
+
\tilde b^2
$$

 plus the cross terms:

 $$
+2\tilde w_1\tilde w_2x_1x_2
+2\tilde w_1\tilde b x_1
+2\tilde w_2\tilde b x_2.
$$

 Now take the expectation:

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
E[\tilde w_1^2]x_1^2
+
E[\tilde w_2^2]x_2^2
+
E[\tilde b^2]
$$

 $$
+2x_1x_2E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
+2x_1E[\tilde w_1\tilde b]
+2x_2E[\tilde w_2\tilde b].
$$

 Now notice:

 $$
E[\tilde w_1^2]
=
\operatorname{Var}(w_1)
$$

 and similarly:

 $$
E[\tilde w_2^2]
=
\operatorname{Var}(w_2),
$$

 $$
E[\tilde b^2]
=
\operatorname{Var}(b).
$$

 So we have

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
x_1^2\operatorname{Var}(w_1)
+x_2^2\operatorname{Var}(w_2)
+\operatorname{Var}(b)
$$

 plus those cross terms.

---

 # 4\. This is where independence matters

 Consider:

 $$
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2].
$$

 Since

 $$
\tilde w_1=w_1-E[w_1]
$$

 and

 $$
\tilde w_2=w_2-E[w_2],
$$

 we have

 $$
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
=
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2).
$$

 If $w_1$ and $w_2$ are independent, then

 $$
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2)=0.
$$

 Therefore

 $$
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]=0.
$$

 And similarly,

 $$
E[\tilde w_1\tilde b]=0,
$$

 $$
E[\tilde w_2\tilde b]=0.
$$

 So **all the cross terms disappear**.

 What's left is

 $$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
x_1^2\operatorname{Var}(w_1)
+
x_2^2\operatorname{Var}(w_2)
+
\operatorname{Var}(b)
}
$$

 and for the general case:

 $$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
\sum_j x_j^2\operatorname{Var}(w_j)
+
\operatorname{Var}(b)
}
$$

 assuming the $w_j$'s and $b$ are independent.

---

 ## 5\. This is slightly different from the statement in Nielsen's hint

 The general rule Nielsen gives you is:

 $$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)
=
\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)
}
$$

 when $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

 You can apply that repeatedly:

 $$
z=
w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\cdots+w_nx_n+b.
$$

 Therefore

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
\operatorname{Var}(w_1x_1)
+
\operatorname{Var}(w_2x_2)
+\cdots+
\operatorname{Var}(w_nx_n)
+
\operatorname{Var}(b).
$$

 And because $x_j$ is a constant,

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(cx)=c^2\operatorname{Var}(x)
$$

 when $c$ is a constant. Therefore:

 $$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(z)
=
\sum_j x_j^2\operatorname{Var}(w_j)
+
\operatorname{Var}(b)
}
$$

 which is the result you need.

---

 ## 6\. Why your mean derivation was useful

 Your reasoning for the mean was:

 $$
E[z]
=
E\left[\sum_jw_jx_j+b\right]
$$

 and linearity of expectation gives

 $$
\boxed{
E[z]
=
\sum_jx_jE[w_j]+E[b].
}
$$

 Notice something interesting:

 **you didn't need independence to prove this.**

 Expectation is always linear:

 $$
E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]
$$

 even if $X$ and $Y$ are correlated.

 Variance is different because of the square:

 $$
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)
=
E[(X+Y-E[X+Y])^2].
$$

 When you expand that square, the cross term

 $$
2E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]
$$

 appears. That's the covariance.

 So the fundamental difference is:

 $$
\boxed{\text{Mean: cross terms don't appear}}
$$

 whereas

 $$
\boxed{\text{Variance: cross terms appear, and independence eliminates them}.}
$$

 This is really the key mathematical idea behind that Nielsen exercise.

 And once you have this, the final part of his exercise — obtaining the standard deviation $\sqrt{3/2}$ — is just plugging in the particular distributions/variances that Nielsen specifies in the preceding paragraph.

 # Covariance derivation

 Absolutely. This is one of those identities that looks obvious after you've seen it, but it's worth deriving carefully.

 Let's start from the definition of the "centered" variables:

 $$
\tilde w_1 = w_1-E[w_1]
$$

 and

 $$
\tilde w_2 = w_2-E[w_2].
$$

 We want to understand why

 $$
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
=
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2).
$$

 ### 1\. Start from the left-hand side

 Substitute the definitions of the tilde variables:

 $$
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
=
E[
(w_1-E[w_1])
(w_2-E[w_2])
].
$$

 Now expand the product:

 $$
=
E[
w_1w_2
-w_1E[w_2]
-E[w_1]w_2
+E[w_1]E[w_2]
].
$$

 ### 2\. Use linearity of expectation

 We can take the expectation term by term:

 $$
=
E[w_1w_2]
-
E[w_1E[w_2]]
-
E[E[w_1]w_2]
+
E[E[w_1]E[w_2]].
$$

 Now remember that $E[w_1]$ and $E[w_2]$ are **numbers**, not random variables. Therefore we can pull them outside the expectation:

 $$
=
E[w_1w_2]
-
E[w_2]E[w_1]
-
E[w_1]E[w_2]
+
E[w_1]E[w_2].
$$

 The second and third terms are the same, so:

 $$
=
E[w_1w_2]
-
E[w_1]E[w_2]
-
E[w_1]E[w_2]
+
E[w_1]E[w_2].
$$

 Therefore:

 $$
\boxed{
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
=
E[w_1w_2]-E[w_1]E[w_2]
}
$$

 But this is **exactly the definition of covariance**:

 $$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2)
=
E[w_1w_2]-E[w_1]E[w_2].
}
$$

 Therefore:

 $$
\boxed{
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
=
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2)
}
$$

---

 ### Another way to see it

 Covariance is actually **defined** as the expectation of the product of the deviations from the mean:

 $$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)
=
E[
(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])
]
}
$$

 So if you define

 $$
\tilde X=X-E[X],
\qquad
\tilde Y=Y-E[Y],
$$

 then you have immediately:

 $$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)
=
E[\tilde X\tilde Y].
$$

 In your neural-network problem:

 $$
\tilde w_j=w_j-E[w_j],
$$

 so

 $$
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2)
=
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2].
$$

 ### And now the connection with independence

 This is why the cross terms in your variance calculation disappear.

 If $w_1$ and $w_2$ are independent, then:

 $$
E[w_1w_2]=E[w_1]E[w_2].
$$

 Therefore:

 $$
\operatorname{Cov}(w_1,w_2)
=
E[w_1w_2]-E[w_1]E[w_2]
=0.
$$

 And consequently:

 $$
\boxed{
E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]=0
}
$$

 which is exactly why the cross term

 $$
2x_1x_2E[\tilde w_1\tilde w_2]
$$

 vanishes in the variance of your neuron's weighted sum.

 One subtle but important point: **independence implies zero covariance, but zero covariance does not in general imply independence.** For this calculation, though, independence is more than enough to make the covariance term vanish.
