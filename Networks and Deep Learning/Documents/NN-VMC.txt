Yes — your recollection of VMC is basically correct. The important thing is that the Monte Carlo part does not disappear when you replace your analytical trial wavefunction with a neural network. The NN simply becomes the new way of representing ψ.

There is one small correction: Metropolis doesn't directly decide whether a configuration is "more probable" in an absolute sense. It uses the ratio of probabilities between the proposed and current configurations.

## The key idea

With your old VMC calculation you had

ψ_α(x)

and therefore

p(x)=|ψ_α(x)|².

You use Metropolis to generate configurations

x₁,x₂,…,x_N

distributed approximately according to

p(x)∝|ψ_α(x)|².

Then you estimate the energy from those samples.

---

## What changes with a neural network?

Almost nothing conceptually.

You simply replace

ψ_α(x)

with

ψ_θ(x)

where θ contains the NN weights and biases.

Therefore your probability distribution becomes

p_θ(x)∝|ψ_θ(x)|².

And this is where Monte Carlo enters the NQS approach.

You use Metropolis (or another Monte Carlo sampler) to generate

x₁,x₂,…,x_N

according to

|ψ_θ(x)|².

Then use those samples to estimate the energy.

---

# The complete NQS + VMC loop

Suppose you randomly initialize the NN:

θ=θ₀.

The NN gives you a trial wavefunction

ψ_{θ₀}(x).

### 1. Generate configurations

Use Metropolis to sample

x∼|ψ_{θ₀}(x)|².

For example:

x₁→x₂→x₃→⋯

where each new configuration is proposed and accepted/rejected according to the Metropolis rule.

### 2. Calculate the energy

For every sampled configuration, calculate the local energy

E_L(x)=Hψ_θ(x)/ψ_θ(x).

Then estimate

E(θ)≈(1/N) Σᵢ E_L(xᵢ)

because the xᵢ's were sampled from |ψ_θ|².

This is the Monte Carlo estimate of the variational energy.

### 3. Calculate how the energy depends on NN parameters

Now you have

E(θ).

You can calculate its gradient

∇_θ E.

### 4. Update the NN

For ordinary gradient descent:

θ←θ−η∇_θ E.

Now the NN represents a new wavefunction:

ψ_{θ_new}(x).

### 5. Sample again

Because the wavefunction changed, the probability distribution changed:

|ψ_{θ_new}(x)|² ≠ |ψ_θ(x)|².

So you run the Monte Carlo sampling again.

And the cycle becomes:

θ
↓
ψ_θ
↓
|ψ_θ|²
↓
Metropolis sampling
↓
x₁,…,x_N
↓
E(θ)
↓
∇_θ E
↓
update θ
↓
repeat

---

## So compare your old VMC with NQS-VMC

### Your old calculation

You had something like

ψ_α(x)

and fixed α.

Then:

ψ_α
→
|ψ_α|²
→
Metropolis
→
E(α).

You repeated this for different α's:

α₁,α₂,α₃,…

and eventually found

α_opt.

### NQS-VMC

Now you have

ψ_θ(x)

where θ might contain thousands of NN parameters.

You don't normally scan all the parameters manually.

Instead:

θ
→
|ψ_θ|²
→
Metropolis
→
E(θ)
→
∇_θ E
→
update θ.

So the NN is essentially replacing your old manual parameter optimization.

---

# And this connects directly to your previous question

You asked before:

> Where does Monte Carlo enter the NN approach?

The answer is:

Monte Carlo is used to evaluate the expectation value of the energy (and its gradient) when the configuration space is too large to integrate explicitly.

The NN does not replace Monte Carlo.

They do different jobs:

Neural network → represents ψ_θ

while

Monte Carlo → samples configurations from |ψ_θ|²

and

Variational principle → provides the energy to minimize.

Then the optimizer (SGD, SR, etc.) changes the NN parameters.

So you can think of NQS-VMC as four pieces working together:

NQS = NN + Monte Carlo + Variational principle + Optimizer

And that is essentially the same VMC you already used, except that your old ψ_α is now replaced by a neural-network wavefunction ψ_θ, and instead of scanning a few α's, you optimize the huge parameter vector θ.



Yes. The approach you're describing is usually called:

 **Neural Quantum States (NQS) + Variational Monte Carlo (VMC)**

 or simply:

 **Neural-network Variational Monte Carlo (NN-VMC)**.

 More specifically:

 - **NQS** = using a neural network to represent the trial wavefunction:\

  $$
  \psi_\theta(x)
  $$
- **VMC** = using the variational principle to minimize the energy.
- **Monte Carlo** = sampling configurations $x$ according to $|\psi_\theta(x)|^2$.
- **SGD / SR / natural gradient** = methods used to optimize the NN parameters $\theta$.

 So your full pipeline is essentially:

 $$
\boxed{
\text{Neural Quantum State}
+
\text{Variational Monte Carlo}
+
\text{NN optimization}
}
$$

 You'll see papers use terms such as **Neural Variational Monte Carlo (Neural VMC)**, **Neural-network quantum states**, **Neural Quantum States (NQS)**, and **deep variational Monte Carlo**. They generally refer to closely related approaches, although the exact NN architecture and optimization method can differ.
